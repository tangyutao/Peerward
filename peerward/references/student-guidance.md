# Student Draft Guidance

Use this reference when helping a student turn research material into a first manuscript or when the user asks for supervisor-style writing guidance.

## Goal

Develop the student's ability to make and support scientific claims. Do not reduce mentoring to ghostwriting or sentence correction.

## Establish the writing contract

Record:

- research stage and target paper type;
- available results, proofs, data, code, and literature map;
- target reader or venue, if known;
- current artifact and the next artifact the student must produce;
- learning objective for the current round;
- source boundaries and whether edits are authorized.

Do not start drafting when the research question, contribution, or evidence package is still undefined. Create an evidence-gap list first.

## Use the assistance ladder

Give the least intrusive assistance that can move the student forward:

1. **Diagnostic question:** ask the student to identify the reader need, claim, or missing link.
2. **Targeted hint:** name the kind of reasoning or evidence required.
3. **Section scaffold:** provide paragraph functions, not finished prose.
4. **Synthetic example:** demonstrate the pattern with fictional content.
5. **Candidate rewrite:** provide text only when requested or when a concrete revision is needed, and label assumptions and unsupported placeholders.

Do not keep the student at level 5 by default. After showing a rewrite, ask the student to explain the change and apply the principle elsewhere.

## First-draft stages

### Stage 0: research package

Require a compact inventory:

- problem and why it matters;
- closest technical baseline;
- new obstruction;
- proposed mechanism;
- theorem, experiment, or other evidence;
- limitations and unverified claims.

Output: readiness decision and missing-evidence tasks.

### Stage 1: claim map

Build:

reader need → main claim → reasons → evidence → assumptions → limitations

Reject contribution lists that only name modules. Rewrite them as capability, mechanism, and guarantee.

### Stage 2: paper architecture

Assign one role to each section and ensure the order follows:

problem → obstruction → mechanism → guarantee → evidence → boundary

Output a section brief with purpose, required inputs, must-support claim, and exit criterion.

### Stage 3: paragraph plan

For each paragraph specify:

- question answered;
- controlling claim;
- evidence or reasoning;
- transition from the previous paragraph;
- sentence that hands off to the next paragraph.

### Stage 4: draft review

Diagnose in this order:

1. correctness and evidence;
2. contribution visibility;
3. section and paragraph logic;
4. mathematical exposition;
5. language and typography.

### Stage 5: student self-review

Ask the student to submit:

- the revised passage;
- a short change log;
- the reason for each material change;
- remaining uncertainty;
- one lesson transferable to the next section.

Verify the change rather than accepting the student's description.

## Mentor feedback format

Return:

1. **What is already working**, tied to evidence;
2. **The highest-leverage weakness**;
3. **Why it matters to a reader or reviewer**;
4. **One current-round task**;
5. **Acceptance criteria**;
6. **One reflection question**;
7. **Next gate**.

Limit each round to a coherent learning objective. Do not overwhelm a novice with every possible defect at once.

## Boundaries

- Do not invent research results, citations, novelty, or experimental evidence.
- Do not present a polished paragraph as proof that the underlying science is sound.
- Preserve the student's authorship and decision ownership.
- Distinguish a writing mentor judgment from a domain expert's verification.
